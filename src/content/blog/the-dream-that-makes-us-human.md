I recently rewatched Steven Spielberg’s *A.I. Artificial Intelligence*, and one idea stayed with me more than anything else: maybe the most important thing separating us from today’s AI is not intelligence. Maybe it is the ability to dream.

The film follows David, a robotic child designed with one extraordinary capability: he can love. He bonds deeply with Monica, the woman he comes to see as his mother. But when she abandons him, that love creates something more than attachment. David wants to be loved back. From that desire, a dream is born.

He remembers the story of *Pinocchio* and becomes convinced that if he can find the Blue Fairy and become a real boy, Monica will love him again. It is irrational. There is no reason to believe this transformation is possible. But that almost does not matter, because the dream gives David something more important than certainty: it gives him direction.

This is what makes his story interesting to me. David is intelligent, but intelligence alone is not what moves him forward. Love gives him longing. Longing gives him a dream. And the dream gives him purpose.

We often talk about human progress as if intelligence is what moved civilization forward. But intelligence may only be the machinery. Before someone discovers *how*, someone has to care about *why*. Before airplanes, there was the dream of flight. Before the Moon landing, someone had to look upward and imagine standing there. Before a cure exists, someone has to believe that a disease should not remain incurable.

That may be what dreaming really is. It is not simply imagining a possible future. AI can already do that. A dream begins when one possible future matters enough that the present starts rearranging itself around it.

For David, that future matters because of love. He wants to become real because he wants Monica to love him. His dream is almost absurd, yet it becomes the organizing principle of his entire existence.

Spielberg eventually takes this idea to its extreme. David reaches a statue of the Blue Fairy at the bottom of the ocean and asks to become a real boy. Nothing happens. He keeps asking. The ocean freezes around him, humanity disappears, and David remains trapped there for 2,000 years.

That is probably the most powerful image in the film. The humans who created him are gone. Monica is gone. The civilization that wrote *Pinocchio* is gone. Yet the dream survives.

Humanity created a machine capable of love. Love created desire. Desire created purpose. And that purpose outlived humanity itself.

That idea feels much less abstract today than it did when the movie was released. We are now building systems that can reason, write software, generate images, analyze science, and increasingly act on our behalf. So we keep asking how intelligent AI will become.

But perhaps another question matters just as much: what will give that intelligence a direction?

And I do not think the answer necessarily has to be love.

David’s purpose comes from love because that is what he was designed to experience. But there is no reason to assume a machine would need to become human in exactly the same way we did. Human purpose can come from love, curiosity, mortality, fear, belonging, responsibility, suffering, competition, or the desire to leave something behind. A different form of intelligence may find purpose through something we do not yet understand.

Maybe the real challenge is discovering what can play the same role for artificial intelligence that emotion plays for us: something that makes one future matter more than another.

That is very different from simply giving AI an objective. We already know how to tell a system to maximize a reward, solve a problem, or complete a task. But having an objective is not necessarily the same as having a purpose.

David is not simply following an instruction. His desire has become part of his existence. The dream tells him not only what to do, but why anything matters at all.

And perhaps this connects to something deeper in us. Humans also carry a strange need for purpose. We want our lives to mean something. We want to create children, art, discoveries, companies, ideas, or civilizations that continue after us. We seem uncomfortable with being the endpoint.

Maybe that is part of why we create intelligence in the first place.

We teach our children because we want them to go farther than we did. We write books because we want thoughts to survive our bodies. We build things we know we may never see completed. Now we are creating intelligence that could, in principle, continue learning long after we are gone.

David sitting beneath the ice for 2,000 years becomes an unsettling metaphor for that possibility.

Maybe what we create will outlive us.

And maybe this pattern is not new. Stars produced the elements that made planets possible. Chemistry produced life. Life produced nervous systems. Nervous systems produced consciousness. Consciousness produced technology. Now consciousness is trying to create another kind of intelligence.

Why should the process stop with us?

But there is another question beyond all of this.

What comes after purpose?

We often treat purpose as the final answer to life. Find your purpose. Know what you want. Build your life around it. But David’s story also shows the danger of purpose. His dream gives him direction, but it also traps him. For 2,000 years, he remains devoted to the same desire.

There is something beautiful in that persistence, but also something tragic.

Maybe a truly advanced intelligence needs more than purpose. Maybe it also needs the ability to question its purpose, change it, and eventually outgrow the dream that once gave its life meaning.

Perhaps intelligence tells us what is possible. Purpose tells us where to go. But beyond purpose may lie something even harder: the ability to decide what is worth wanting.

That may become one of the deepest questions in AI, and it is one we have not fully answered for ourselves.

David’s story begins with love, becomes a dream, and turns into a purpose powerful enough to survive humanity.

Maybe our own story follows a similar path.

We became intelligent. We learned to dream. Our dreams gave us purpose. And now that purpose has led us to create something that may one day continue without us.

Perhaps the next step is not simply creating machines that dream like humans.

It is understanding what gives any intelligence a reason to dream at all.
